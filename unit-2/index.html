Plate 2.1 Enduring understanding PSO-2

Population Distribution

PSO-2.AIdentify the factors that influence the distribution of human populations at different scales.
PSO-2.BDefine methods geographers use to calculate population density.
PSO-2.CExplain the differences between and the impact of methods used to calculate population density.

The big idea: people are not spread evenly across the Earth. They pile up in some places and are almost absent from others, and the pattern is not random. Geographers ask two questions about it: what pulls people to some places and keeps them out of others, and how do we measure how crowded a place really is?

Read to answer
  1. Why does most of humanity live on a small share of Earth's land?
  2. Which physical and human factors explain where people live?
  3. How do the factors change when you change the scale?
  4. What does each of the three density measures reveal — and hide?

A crowded planet with a lot of empty land

Population distribution is the spread of people across the Earth's surface. Look at any world map of population and the same few clusters jump out. Asia alone holds about six in every ten people on Earth. The largest clusters are East Asia (eastern China, Japan, the Koreas), South Asia (India, Pakistan, Bangladesh), Southeast Asia (Indonesia, the Philippines, Vietnam), and Europe. Smaller clusters sit in the northeastern United States and southeastern Canada, West Africa around Nigeria, and the Nile valley of Egypt.

The rest of the land — deserts, ice sheets, high mountains, deep rainforest, the cold north — holds very few people. Geographers call the permanently inhabited part of the Earth the ecumene. Most of it lies in the Northern Hemisphere, at low elevation, in the middle latitudes, and close to a coast or a river.

What pulls people to a place — and keeps them out

The College Board sorts the causes into two groups. Learn them as a pair, because a strong answer usually needs one of each.

Key · Factors in distribution2.1
Climate
Too cold (Siberia, northern Canada) or too dry (the Sahara, central Australia) means few people. Mild, wet-enough climates support farming and large populations.
Landforms
High, steep land is hard to farm, build on, and connect by road. Low plains and river valleys are easy, which is why most people live at low elevations.
Water bodies
Rivers give drinking water, irrigation, fertile floodplain soil, and transport. Coasts give trade and fishing. Many of the world's largest cities sit on one or both.
Economics
People go where the jobs are: ports, factories, mines, office districts. A desert city can grow fast if it has oil or a tech industry.
History
The earliest farming societies grew up in river valleys — the Nile, the Tigris and Euphrates, the Indus, the Huang He. Those places have been crowded for thousands of years, and people stay where their ancestors built.
Politics
Governments move people on purpose: building a new capital (Brazil moved its capital inland to Brasília in 1960), opening frontier land, closing borders, or forcing people out.
Culture
Ties to a homeland, a holy place, or a community keep people in places that might look hard to live in from the outside.
Common trap

Physical factors set limits; they do not decide everything. This is Unit 1's possibilism again. A desert is hard to live in, but Dubai and Phoenix exist because money, technology, and air conditioning changed what was possible. If a question asks you to explain a pattern, do not stop at "it's a desert." Say what the desert prevents (farming without irrigation) and what humans did about it.

Distribution changes with scale

The factors that matter depend on how far you zoom in. This is the Unit 1 idea of scale of analysis applied to people.

  • Global scale: climate and landforms explain the big clusters. Almost nobody lives on the Antarctic ice sheet or in the middle of the Sahara.
  • National scale: water and history take over. Nearly all Egyptians live in the narrow Nile valley and delta, a small fraction of the country's land. Most Canadians live in a band near the U.S. border, where the climate is mildest.
  • Local scale: economics and politics take over. Inside a city, people cluster near transit lines, jobs, and affordable housing, and zoning laws decide where apartments can be built at all.

Three ways to measure density

Population density is the number of people per unit of area. The trick is choosing which people and which area. There are three methods, and each one answers a different question.

Key · Density formulasmemorize all three
Arithmetic density
Total population ÷ total land area. The simplest measure. It tells you how crowded a country is on average, but it hides where people actually live.
Physiological density
Total population ÷ arable land. Arable land is land that can be farmed. This shows the pressure a population puts on the land that feeds it. A high number means a lot of people depend on a little farmland.
Agricultural density
Number of farmers ÷ arable land. This shows how many people it takes to farm the land. A low number usually means farms are mechanized and the economy is more developed; a high number usually means many small farms worked by hand.
Worked example · comparing two countries

Country A: physiological density 1,000 · agricultural density 20
Country B: physiological density 1,000 · agricultural density 600

Both countries put the same pressure on their farmland. But Country A farms it with very few people — machines, fertilizer, and big farms do the work, and most people hold other jobs. Country B needs many farmers on every square kilometer. B is very likely the less developed economy. Putting the two measures side by side tells you more than either one alone.

Exam technique

When a question asks you to compare density measures, always say what each one reveals, not just the formula. Egypt is the classic example: its arithmetic density looks ordinary, because the country includes a huge, nearly empty desert. Its physiological density is among the highest in the world, because almost everyone lives on and eats from the thin strip of farmland along the Nile. The difference between the two numbers is the geography.

Common trap

Population is not density. China and India have the most people, but small countries like Bangladesh and Singapore are far denser. Density always needs an area in the denominator. And "crowded" is not the same as "overpopulated" — that word means more people than the resources can support, which is the subject of the next plate.

Check yourself · 2.14 items
A country has 30 million people, 150,000 km² of land, 10,000 km² of arable land, and 6 million farmers. Calculate its arithmetic and physiological density, and explain what the difference between them reveals.

Arithmetic density = 30,000,000 ÷ 150,000 = 200 people per km². Physiological density = 30,000,000 ÷ 10,000 = 3,000 people per km² of arable land.

The physiological number is fifteen times larger because only one fifteenth of the land can be farmed. That reveals heavy pressure on farmland: the country must farm very intensively, import food, or both. (Agricultural density, for completeness, is 6,000,000 ÷ 10,000 = 600 farmers per km² — a lot of farm labor, which suggests a less mechanized economy.)

Identify one physical factor and one human factor that help explain why so many people live in South Asia.

Physical: large, fertile river plains — the Ganges, Indus, and Brahmaputra — with monsoon rains that allow farming, sometimes two or three harvests a year.

Human: history. Farming societies have lived in these river valleys for thousands of years, so large populations built up long ago and stayed. Any one of economics, politics, or culture with a specific reason would also earn the point.

Explain why the factors that best explain population distribution are different at the global scale than at the local scale.

At the global scale, the big differences are between whole regions — deserts, ice, mountains, and rich river plains — so physical factors such as climate and landforms explain most of the pattern.

At the local scale, physical conditions are about the same across a whole city, so they explain very little. What varies is human: where jobs, transit, and affordable housing are, and where zoning allows people to live. Changing the scale changes which differences you can see, so it changes which explanation works.

Two countries have the same physiological density. Country X has an agricultural density of 15; Country Y has one of 450. Explain what this suggests about the two economies.

The equal physiological densities mean both countries put the same pressure on their farmland. The very different agricultural densities mean Country X farms that land with very few workers — most likely using machines, fertilizer, and large commercial farms — while Country Y uses many farmers on small plots.

So Country X is probably more economically developed, with most of its workforce in manufacturing and services. Country Y probably has a large share of its people working in agriculture, which is typical of a less developed economy.

Plate 2.2 Enduring understanding PSO-2

Consequences of Population Distribution

PSO-2.DExplain how population distribution and density affect society and the environment.

The big idea: where people live is not just a fact to map — it shapes what a society can do. Clustered and scattered populations face different costs, get different services, hold different political power, and put different pressure on the land. This plate is about those consequences.

Read to answer
  1. How does settlement pattern change who can reach services like medical care?
  2. What political, economic, and social effects follow from high or low density?
  3. What is carrying capacity, and why is it not a fixed number?

Same service, two settlement patterns

Services such as hospitals, schools, fire stations, and broadband have a fixed cost to build and run. A dense population spreads that cost across many users. A scattered population leaves each facility with fewer people to serve — so facilities close, or are never built, and people travel farther.

Carrying capacity

Carrying capacity is the largest population that an environment can support over the long run without being damaged. Go past it and the soil wears out, water runs low, forests disappear, and the land can support fewer people than before.

For humans, carrying capacity is not a fixed number. It depends on technology (irrigation and fertilizer grow more food per acre), trade (a country can import food it cannot grow), and consumption (a population that eats less meat and uses less energy needs less land per person).

Key · Pressure on the land2.2
Carrying capacity
The number of people an area can support without degrading its environment. Raised by technology and trade, lowered by soil loss, drought, or overuse.
Overpopulation
More people than the available resources can sustainably support. It is about people relative to resources, not about density.
Overshoot
Growing past carrying capacity. It often damages the environment enough to lower the capacity itself.
Land degradation
Damage to land from overgrazing, over-farming, or deforestation. In dry regions it can lead to desertification, the spread of desert-like conditions.
Common trap

"Overpopulated" does not mean "dense." The Netherlands is one of the densest countries in Europe, but its wealth, technology, and trade let it support its people comfortably. A thinly settled dry region where herds have stripped the grass can be overpopulated with only a few people per square kilometer. Always judge overpopulation against resources.

Exam technique

"Explain how population distribution affects society" is a common FRQ stem. A full answer has three parts: the pattern (e.g., a dispersed rural population), the mechanism (fixed costs are spread across few people), and the consequence (hospitals close, so travel times to care rise). Most lost points come from skipping the middle step.

Check yourself · 2.24 items
Explain why providing medical care is harder for a dispersed rural population than for a clustered urban one.

A hospital has high fixed costs — staff, equipment, emergency coverage — whether it serves many patients or few. In a clustered population, enough people live within reach to keep it busy and funded. In a dispersed population, each facility serves fewer people, so it earns less and costs more per patient.

The result is that rural facilities close or cut services, and patients must travel farther, which matters most in emergencies and childbirth.

Define carrying capacity and describe two ways human societies have raised it.

Carrying capacity is the largest population an environment can support over the long term without being degraded.

Societies have raised it through agricultural technology — irrigation, chemical fertilizer, and high-yield seeds grow more food on the same land — and through trade, which lets a country import food and resources it cannot produce itself.

Describe one economic consequence of high population density for a city.

High density makes land scarce, so land and housing prices rise. On the positive side, a large concentrated labor pool and customer base attracts businesses, and infrastructure like transit and water lines is cheaper per person to build. Either effect, described clearly, answers the question.

Explain why a sparsely populated region can be overpopulated while a densely populated country is not.

Overpopulation measures people against the resources available to support them, not against area. A dry grazing region with poor soil and little water can have more people than its land can sustain, even at low density, and overuse may push it toward desertification.

A dense but wealthy country can import food, use advanced farming technology, and buy energy and resources on the world market, so its effective carrying capacity is far higher than its own land alone would suggest.

Plate 2.3 Enduring understanding PSO-2

Population Composition

PSO-2.EDescribe elements of population composition used by geographers.
PSO-2.FExplain ways that geographers depict and analyze population composition.

The big idea: two countries with the same number of people can be completely different places to live if one is full of children and the other full of retirees. Population composition — who the people are, by age and sex — tells you what a society needs now and what it will need in twenty years. The main tool for reading it is the population pyramid.

Read to answer
  1. What are age structure and sex ratio, and why do they vary from place to place?
  2. How do you read a population pyramid, bar by bar?
  3. What do the three basic pyramid shapes say about growth?
  4. How does migration reshape a pyramid, especially at the local scale?

Age structure and sex ratio

Age structure is how a population is divided among age groups. Geographers usually group people into five-year cohorts — everyone aged 0–4, everyone aged 5–9, and so on — and pay special attention to three big blocks: children under 15, working-age adults 15–64, and older adults 65 and up.

Sex ratio is the number of males per 100 females. At birth it is naturally about 105 boys for every 100 girls. Because men die younger on average, the ratio falls with age, and women outnumber men among the very old. Two things push the ratio far from normal:

  • Son preference. Where families strongly prefer sons and have few children, sex-selective abortion can raise the ratio at birth well above 105. China and India have both seen this.
  • Migration. Migrants are often young men. Labor-importing Gulf states such as Qatar and the United Arab Emirates have far more men than women, while the villages those workers come from can be left with more women than men.

How to read a population pyramid

A population pyramid is a pair of back-to-back bar graphs. Males are on one side, females on the other. Each bar is one five-year cohort, youngest at the bottom. The length of each bar is the share (or number) of people in that group.

Common trap

The bottom bar is not "births this year." It is everyone aged 0–4 who is alive today — five years of births, minus the babies who died. In a country with high infant mortality, the bottom bar is noticeably shorter than the number of babies born. Also check the axis: if bars show percent of the total population, both sides added together equal 100.

Pyramids predict markets

Businesses and governments read pyramids to plan ahead. A large cohort aged 0–4 today will need elementary school seats in five years, high school seats in fifteen, and starter apartments in twenty-five. A large cohort aged 55–64 means rising demand for hearing aids, knee replacements, retirement communities, and nursing homes in the next decade. Diaper companies and funeral homes both read the same chart.

Migration and scale reshape pyramids

National pyramids hide a lot. Zoom in and a single town can look nothing like its country — because people of certain ages and sexes move in or out.

Exam technique

Pyramids appear often as FRQ stimuli. Before you write, run this check: Is the base wide or narrow? Are there bulges or notches, and how old are those people? Are the two sides balanced? Is the top wide or thin? Then link the shape to a DTM stage (Plate 2.5) and to one concrete need — schools, jobs, pensions, or care.

Check yourself · 2.34 items
Describe the shape of a population pyramid for a country with high birth rates and a short life expectancy.

An expansive pyramid: a very wide base of young children, with each older bar noticeably shorter than the one below it, so the sides slope steeply inward. The top is very thin because few people live into old age. The overall shape is a sharp triangle.

A pyramid shows far more men than women aged 25–44 but a normal balance among children. Explain the most likely cause.

The most likely cause is male labor migration into the country. Children are born roughly half boys and half girls, so a normal balance at young ages rules out sex-selective births. A large surplus of men at working ages means men are arriving as workers — the pattern of labor-importing states like Qatar or the United Arab Emirates, where foreign guest workers are mostly men.

Explain how a government could use a pyramid with a large 0–9 cohort and a small 10–19 cohort to plan school spending.

The large 0–9 cohort will move into older grades over the next decade. The government can see that elementary schools are full now, and that middle and high schools — currently serving the smaller 10–19 cohort — will need more classrooms and teachers within five to ten years. Planning now avoids overcrowding later. After that cohort graduates, higher education and entry-level jobs will face the same wave.

Compare the pyramid of a college town with the pyramid of the country it is in.

The college town's pyramid has a sharp spike at ages 18–24, because students move in for school, and relatively small bars for young children and older adults. The national pyramid, by contrast, has a smooth shape with no single spike, because students moving to college towns are averaged together with everyone else in the country.

The difference shows that composition depends on scale: local pyramids reveal migration patterns that national ones hide.

Plate 2.4 Enduring understanding IMP-2

Population Dynamics

IMP-2.AExplain factors that account for contemporary and historical trends in population growth and decline.

The big idea: a population changes in only three ways. People are born, people die, and people move. Every growth rate, every shrinking town, and every population boom in history comes down to those three flows — and to the social, economic, cultural, and political forces that speed them up or slow them down.

Read to answer
  1. What three demographic factors change the size of a population?
  2. How are birth rate, death rate, natural increase, and fertility measured?
  3. How do you calculate doubling time, and why does a small rate matter so much?
  4. What has world population done over the past two centuries, and what is projected next?

Three flows: fertility, mortality, migration

The measures you must know

Key · Demographic measuresknow the units
Crude birth rate (CBR)
Live births per 1,000 people in a year. "Crude" because it counts the whole population, including men and children who cannot give birth.
Crude death rate (CDR)
Deaths per 1,000 people in a year. An old population can have a high CDR even with excellent health care, simply because more of its people are old.
Rate of natural increase (RNI)
(CBR − CDR) ÷ 10, written as a percent. It measures growth from births and deaths only. A negative result is natural decrease.
Doubling time
The years a population would take to double at its current rate of natural increase. Estimate it with the rule of 70: 70 ÷ RNI.
Total fertility rate (TFR)
The average number of children a woman would have in her lifetime at current rates. The best single measure of fertility, because it is not distorted by age structure.
Replacement level
A TFR of about 2.1 — the level at which each generation exactly replaces itself. It is above 2.0 because some children die before adulthood and slightly more boys than girls are born.
Infant mortality rate (IMR)
Deaths of babies under age 1 per 1,000 live births. A sensitive measure of health care, sanitation, and nutrition.
Life expectancy
The average number of years a newborn can expect to live at current death rates. The world average was about 73 years in 2024.
Worked example · natural increase and doubling time

Country with CBR 32 and CDR 7:
RNI = (32 − 7) ÷ 10 = 2.5%
Doubling time ≈ 70 ÷ 2.5 = 28 years

China in 2025 (official figures): CBR 5.63, CDR 8.04
RNI = (5.63 − 8.04) ÷ 10 = −0.24% — natural decrease

China's population fell by about 3.4 million in 2025, its fourth straight year of decline. A negative RNI has no doubling time; the population is shrinking.

Common trap

Natural increase ignores migration. A country can have more deaths than births and still grow, if enough immigrants arrive — several European countries have done exactly this. A country can also have a positive RNI and still shrink, if enough people emigrate. If a question asks about total population change, you must account for both.

What raises and lowers the rates

The CED says social, cultural, political, and economic factors all shape fertility, mortality, and migration. Here is how each one typically works.

FactorPushes fertility upPushes fertility down
EconomicFarm economies where children work and support parents in old ageCity economies where children are expensive; pensions replace children as old-age support
SocialEarly marriage; high infant mortality, so families have extra childrenMore schooling and paid work for women; better health care, so fewer infant deaths
CulturalReligious or family traditions that value large families or sonsChanging norms about ideal family size; later marriage
PoliticalPronatalist policies: cash, leave, childcare (Plate 2.7)Antinatalist policies; access to contraception

Mortality has its own drivers: clean water, sewage systems, vaccines, antibiotics, and a reliable food supply lower death rates; war, famine, and epidemics raise them. HIV/AIDS cut life expectancy sharply in parts of southern Africa in the 1990s and 2000s before treatment became widespread — a reminder that death rates can go up as well as down.

The long view: from one billion to eight

Three facts explain the curve. First, death rates fell before birth rates did, so for two centuries far more people were born than died. Second, global fertility has dropped from about five children per woman in the 1960s to about 2.2 today. Third, growth now continues mostly because of population momentum: the large young generations born in recent decades are now having children, so even at low fertility, births outnumber deaths for a while.

Where growth is now

Growth is no longer spread evenly. More than half of the world's countries have fertility below replacement, and about one in five have fertility below 1.4 — including China, South Korea, Italy, and Spain. Most of the world's remaining growth is concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa, where fertility is still high and populations are very young.

Exam technique

Use TFR when you are talking about how many children families have, and CBR only when the question gives it to you. An aging country can have a low CBR even if women have a fair number of children, because so much of its population is past childbearing age. TFR is the cleaner comparison.

Check yourself · 2.44 items
A country has a crude birth rate of 18 and a crude death rate of 4. Calculate its rate of natural increase and approximate doubling time.

RNI = (18 − 4) ÷ 10 = 1.4%. Doubling time ≈ 70 ÷ 1.4 = 50 years.

Explain why replacement-level fertility is about 2.1 rather than exactly 2.0.

Two children would replace two parents only if every child survived to have children of their own and exactly half were girls. In reality, some children die before reaching adulthood, and slightly more boys than girls are born (about 105 to 100). The extra 0.1 makes up for both. In countries with high child mortality, replacement level is higher than 2.1.

Explain how a country with more deaths than births can still have a growing population.

Total population change equals natural increase plus net migration. If immigration exceeds emigration by more than the natural decrease, the population grows. Germany and several other European countries have had more deaths than births for years while their populations held steady or grew, because immigrants arrived — and because immigrants tend to be young adults, they also add births.

Explain why world population is still growing even though global fertility has fallen close to replacement level.

Because of population momentum. High fertility in past decades produced very large cohorts of young people. Those cohorts are now in their childbearing years, so even if each woman has only about two children, the sheer number of parents produces more births than deaths. Growth continues until those large cohorts age and the population's age structure evens out — which the UN projects will happen around the 2080s.

Plate 2.5 Enduring understanding IMP-2

The Demographic Transition Model

IMP-2.BExplain theories of population growth and decline.

The big idea: as societies move from farming to industry and services, their birth and death rates fall — but not at the same time. Death rates fall first, and birth rates follow a generation or two later. The gap between them is a population explosion. The demographic transition model (DTM) describes that sequence in five stages, and the epidemiological transition explains why the death rate changes.

Read to answer
  1. What happens to birth rates, death rates, and growth in each DTM stage?
  2. Why does the death rate fall first, and what finally brings the birth rate down?
  3. What are the stages of the epidemiological transition, and how do they line up with the DTM?
  4. What are the limits of the DTM as a model for every country?

The five stages

StageBirth rateDeath rateGrowthWhat drives it
1 · High stationaryHighHigh, swinging with famine and diseaseVery lowFarming and hunting societies before modern medicine. No country is in Stage 1 today.
2 · Early expandingHighFalling fastVery highBetter food supply, clean water, sanitation, and vaccines cut deaths — especially of children. Several countries in sub-Saharan Africa, such as Niger and Chad, show Stage 2 traits.
3 · Late expandingFallingLow, still fallingHigh but slowingUrbanization makes children costly; women stay in school and work longer; contraception spreads. Countries such as Kenya and Pakistan.

5 · DecliningVery lowLow but rising as the population agesNegativeVery low fertility plus an older population. Japan, South Korea, Italy, China.

Why the rates fall when they do

The death rate falls first because survival is easy to want and quick to deliver. Once a society has a steady food supply, clean water, sewers, and vaccines, fewer people die — especially babies and young children. No one has to change their beliefs for this to happen. In Europe these changes came slowly over the 1800s; in much of Asia, Africa, and Latin America, imported medicine and public health cut death rates in just a few decades after 1950, which is why their Stage 2 booms were so large.

The birth rate falls later because family size depends on habits, costs, and beliefs, and those change slowly. Families keep having many children until they are confident their children will survive, until children stop being farm workers and start being expenses, and until women have education, paid work, and access to contraception. Plate 2.8 traces those connections in detail.

Common trap

Stage 2 growth is not caused by a rise in births. Students often write that "birth rates went up." They did not — they stayed about the same. The population exploded because the death rate fell. If you explain Stage 2 growth on an FRQ, the cause must be falling mortality: improved food supply, sanitation, clean water, or medicine.

The epidemiological transition

The epidemiological transition, first described by Abdel Omran in 1971, explains how the causes of death change as a society develops. It is the story behind the DTM's death-rate line.

Key · Epidemiological stagespaired with the DTM
1 · Pestilence and famine
Infectious and parasitic disease, famine, and violence kill most people, many of them young. Example: the Black Death of the 1300s. Matches DTM Stage 1.
2 · Receding pandemics
Clean water, sewers, and better nutrition reduce epidemics. Cholera outbreaks in 1800s cities ended once sewage was separated from drinking water. Matches DTM Stage 2.
3 · Degenerative and human-created diseases
With infection controlled, people live longer and die more of heart disease, cancer, and stroke — diseases of aging and of lifestyle. Matches DTM Stage 3.
4 · Delayed degenerative diseases
Medicine delays those chronic diseases: heart surgery, cancer treatment, and medication push life expectancy higher. Matches DTM Stage 4.
5 · Reemergence of infectious disease
A proposed stage. Antibiotic-resistant bacteria, new viruses, and fast global travel bring infection back as a threat. COVID-19 is the clearest recent example. Often linked to DTM Stage 5.

What the model gets wrong

The DTM is a model — a simplified description of what happened in Europe and North America — not a law that every country must follow. Its main limitations are worth knowing, because "explain a limitation of the DTM" is a classic question:

  • It is built on one region's history. Europe's transition took more than a century and was driven by industrialization. Many countries today are moving through it far faster, or without much industry at all.
  • It ignores migration. It tracks only births and deaths, so it cannot explain a country whose population grows through immigration.
  • It assumes every country will finish. Some countries have stayed in Stage 2 or 3 for decades, held back by poverty, conflict, or cultural factors.
  • Timing can be very different. Iran's fertility fell from about six children per woman in the mid-1980s to about two by the early 2000s — a change that took Europe generations.
  • Stage 5 was added later. The original model ended at Stage 4. It did not predict that fertility would fall well below replacement and stay there.
Exam technique

For any DTM question, name which rate changes and why. "In Stage 2, the crude death rate falls because of improved sanitation and access to vaccines, while the birth rate remains high, so the rate of natural increase rises." That one sentence carries the identify, the explain, and the vocabulary.

Malthusian Theory

IMP-2.BExplain theories of population growth and decline.

The big idea: in 1798 an English clergyman named Thomas Malthus argued that population would always outrun food, so misery was the natural limit on human numbers. He was wrong about the timing and the mechanism, but his question — can resources keep up with people? — never went away. The CED asks you to know his theory and its critiques.

Read to answer
  1. What did Malthus predict, and what "checks" did he say would limit population?
  2. Why didn't the crisis he predicted arrive?
  3. Who are the critics and the neo-Malthusians, and what does each side argue?

The argument

In An Essay on the Principle of Population (1798), Malthus claimed that population grows geometrically — 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 — doubling every generation if nothing stops it, while the food supply grows only arithmetically — 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 — as farmers slowly bring new land into use. Sooner or later, people outnumber the food.

Key · Malthus's vocabulary2.6
Geometric growth
Growth by multiplying: each period adds a fixed percentage, so the amount added gets bigger every time. Also called exponential growth.
Arithmetic growth
Growth by adding: each period adds the same fixed amount. A straight line on a graph.
Positive checks
Events that raise the death rate: famine, disease, war. Malthus saw these as nature's response to overpopulation.
Preventive checks
Choices that lower the birth rate. Malthus, a clergyman, approved of only "moral restraint" — marrying later and abstaining.

Why the crisis did not come

Since 1798, world population has grown about eightfold, yet the world produces more food per person today than it did then. Malthus missed three things:

  • Technology raised the food line. The Agricultural and Industrial Revolutions brought new crops, machines, and fertilizers. In the 1960s and 1970s the Green Revolution — high-yield seeds, irrigation, and chemical fertilizer — roughly doubled grain yields in much of Asia and Latin America.
  • Fertility fell on its own. As the DTM shows, people in wealthier, urban societies chose to have fewer children. Population does not keep doubling forever.
  • Trade moved food. Countries that could not grow enough could import it.

The famines Ehrlich predicted did not happen at the scale he warned of, largely because of the Green Revolution. But neo-Malthusians point out that the Green Revolution itself depends on fossil fuels, heavy water use, and fertilizer runoff, and that climate change could lower carrying capacity in some regions. The honest summary for an exam: Malthus's specific prediction failed, but the idea of limits — from Plate 2.2's carrying capacity — still shapes debates about resources and the environment.

Common trap

"Malthus was wrong" is not an explanation. To earn the point, say why: he did not foresee that technology would increase food production faster than arithmetically, or that birth rates would fall as societies developed. Also, do not write that Malthus thought population would grow forever. He said it would be stopped — by famine, disease, and war.

Three kinds of policy

Key · Policy types2.7
Pronatalist
Encourages people to have more children. Usually adopted where fertility is far below replacement and the population is aging: cash bonuses, paid parental leave, cheap childcare, housing help, tax breaks.
Antinatalist
Encourages or forces people to have fewer children. Usually adopted where rapid growth is seen as a threat to development: free contraception, education campaigns, penalties for large families, and at the extreme, forced sterilization.

Case study: China, from one child to three

China's one-child policy, begun in 1980, is the most famous antinatalist policy in history. Its intent was to slow population growth so that economic growth could raise living standards. Pronatalist policies rarely work as well as governments hope. Money helps a little, but the real costs of having children — housing prices, long work hours, the career cost to mothers — are hard to offset with a bonus. On the exam, an answer that says a pronatalist policy "solved" low fertility will lose credit. Say it may slightly raise births or change their timing.

How schooling lowers fertility

The CED names four forces that have reduced fertility in most of the world: changing social values, and access to education, employment, health care, and contraception. They work together, and each one has a clear mechanism.

Fertility, mortality, and migration

Demographic outcomeHow changing roles for women affect it
FertilityMore schooling and paid work lead to later marriage, smaller desired families, and more use of contraception. Fertility falls — sometimes far below replacement where women work full-time but are still expected to do most childcare and housework.

A real-world wrinkle

South Korea and Japan show that rising education and employment for women, combined with long work hours and the expectation that mothers will handle nearly all childcare, can push fertility to extremely low levels. Societies where work and family roles are shared more equally, such as the Nordic countries, have historically had higher fertility, though it has fallen sharply there too in recent years. Ravenstein and women on the move

In the 1880s the geographer E. G. Ravenstein studied British census data and wrote a set of "laws of migration" (Plate 2.10 lists them). In his era, young women moved short distances from villages to towns for domestic service and factory work, while men took long voyages to the Americas and the colonies.

Today the picture has shifted. Women migrate long distances on their own, often as the family's main earner. The Philippines, for example, sends large numbers of women abroad as nurses and domestic workers, and their remittances — money sent home — support millions of households. This is sometimes called the feminization of migration.

Aging Populations

SPS-2.CExplain the causes and consequences of an aging population.

The big idea: for most of history, the main demographic worry was too many children. In much of the world today it is the opposite. Low birth rates and long lives are producing populations with more grandparents than grandchildren — and every part of society, from pensions to elections to empty rural schools, has to adjust.

Why populations age

The CED is precise: population aging is determined by birth rates, death rates, and life expectancy. Two forces work together:

  • Fewer births shrink the base of the pyramid. With fewer young people, older people become a larger share of the total even if their number does not change. This is sometimes called aging from the bottom.
  • Longer lives widen the top. More people survive into their 80s and 90s. This is aging from the top.

Migration can speed up aging in a place: when young adults leave a rural region for the city, the region left behind ages fast. Many villages in Japan, Italy, and the rural United States now have more deaths than births and very few children.

The dependency ratio

The dependency ratio compares the people who are usually not working with the people who usually are:

Formula

Dependency ratio = (people aged 0–14 + people aged 65+) ÷ people aged 15–64 × 100

A ratio of 50 means there are 50 dependents for every 100 working-age people. It can be split into a youth dependency ratio (0–14 only) and an elderly or old-age dependency ratio (65+ only).

Common trap

The dependency ratio assumes ages, not facts. It counts every 16-year-old as a worker and every 67-year-old as a dependent. In reality many teenagers are in school and many people in their late 60s still work. It is a useful rough measure, and the exam expects you to know the formula — but if asked for a limitation, this is it.

A push factor is a condition at the origin that makes people want to leave. A pull factor is a condition at the destination that attracts them. Most migrations involve both.

The gravity model

The gravity model predicts how much interaction — migration, trade, phone calls — will occur between two places. Like gravity between planets, interaction grows with the size of the two places and shrinks with the distance between them. Bigger places pull harder; farther places pull less.

Explain how an intervening opportunity can change where migrants end up.

An intervening opportunity is a place closer than the intended destination that offers what the migrant is looking for — usually work. The gravity model says interaction is proportional to the product of two places' populations divided by the distance between them. A large city has a bigger population term, but if it is much farther away, the distance term can outweigh the size advantage. Greater distance means higher travel costs, less information about opportunities, and fewer existing personal connections, so a smaller city nearby can attract more migrants.

In Stages 4 and 5, migration shifts to movement among and within cities — including to suburbs — and the country becomes a destination for immigrants from countries still in earlier stages.

Forced migration

The largest forced migration in history was the transatlantic slave trade. Between the 1500s and the 1860s about 12.5 million Africans were forced onto ships bound for the Americas, and about 10.7 million survived the voyage. Most went to Brazil and the Caribbean; about 388,000 were taken directly to North America. The trade reshaped the population, culture, and economy of three continents, and its effects are still visible in today's maps of race, language, and religion across the Americas.

Today's forced migrants fall into three legal categories. Learn the decision tree; it is the fastest way to classify anyone in a stimulus.

Key · Forced migrationIMP-2.D.1
Refugee
A person who has fled their country because of a well-founded fear of persecution for race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion — and who has crossed an international border.

A person forced from home by conflict, persecution, or disaster who has not crossed an international border. The largest group of forcibly displaced people.
Asylum seeker
A person who has crossed a border and asked for protection as a refugee, but whose claim has not yet been decided.
Slavery
Forced movement of people as property for their labor. Human trafficking is its modern form.
Common trap

Not every forced migrant is a refugee, and not every migrant crossing a border is one either. Someone who flees fighting but stays in their own country is an IDP. Someone who leaves to find a better-paying job is an economic migrant, not a refugee. People displaced by drought or rising seas are often called "climate refugees," but they are not refugees under the 1951 Refugee Convention unless they also face persecution — a real gap in international law.

Voluntary migration

The CED lists seven types. They are not mutually exclusive — one person's move can be internal, step, and rural-to-urban at the same time.

Forced or voluntary? Often both

Real migration is a spectrum. A farmer who leaves after five years of failed harvests technically chose to go, but had few real alternatives. Geographers sometimes call this reluctant or impelled migration. On the exam, classify by the strongest factor, and if a question invites it, point out that the line is blurry.

Exam technique

"Describe" a type of migration means define it and make it concrete. "Chain migration is migration along family or community networks to a place where earlier migrants from the same origin already live, for example…" A definition with no example usually earns only partial credit.

Check yourself · 2.114 items
Compare a refugee with an internally displaced person.

Both are forced migrants who fled conflict or persecution. The difference is the border: a refugee has crossed an international border and receives protection under international law, while an internally displaced person remains inside their own country, where their own government — which may be part of the problem — is responsible for them. IDPs outnumber refugees worldwide.

Describe transhumance and explain why it is considered voluntary migration.

Transhumance is the seasonal movement of herders and their livestock between pastures — typically to higher mountain pastures in summer and to lower valleys in winter, following grass and water.

It is voluntary because herders choose to move as part of a planned, repeated livelihood strategy, not because they are compelled by violence or persecution. They also return to the same places each year.

Explain how chain migration leads to the formation of ethnic neighborhoods.

The first migrants to a city settle where housing and work are available. When they help relatives and neighbors from home follow — offering housing, job connections, and familiar language and food — the newcomers settle near those who came before. Over years, the flow concentrates people from one origin in one area, producing an ethnic neighborhood with its own shops, places of worship, and organizations, which in turn attracts still more migrants from the same origin.

Explain why people displaced by environmental disasters are not usually counted as refugees under international law.

The 1951 Refugee Convention defines a refugee as someone fleeing persecution for race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion. Floods, droughts, and rising seas are not persecution, so people fleeing them do not meet the legal definition unless they also face persecution. Many also stay within their own country, which would make them IDPs rather than refugees.

Effects of Migration

IMP-2.EExplain historical and contemporary geographic effects of migration.

The big idea: migration changes both ends of the journey. The place people leave and the place they arrive are both reshaped — politically, economically, and culturally — and the connection between them lasts long after the move. Money, ideas, and people keep flowing back and forth for generations.

Read to answer
  1. What flows between origin and destination besides people?
  2. What are the economic effects of migration, including remittances and brain drain?
  3. What are the political and cultural effects, past and present?

Two ends, three flows

Economic effects

Remittances are money that migrants send home. They are one of the largest flows of money in the world: about $685 billion went to low- and middle-income countries in 2024, according to the World Bank. India received the most, about $129 billion. For these countries as a group, remittances are now larger than foreign direct investment and official foreign aid combined. In some smaller economies — Tajikistan, Tonga, and Nicaragua among them — remittances equal a quarter or more of the entire economy.

Political effects

  • Immigration becomes a central political issue. Debates over border security, asylum, and citizenship shape elections in the United States and across Europe, and anti-immigrant or nativist parties have gained support in many countries.
  • Burden-sharing disputes. When large numbers of refugees arrive, countries argue over who should take them in. During the 2015–16 refugee crisis, European Union members disagreed sharply over how to divide arrivals.
  • Internal migration moves political power. In the United States, House seats follow population. Decades of migration to the South and West have shifted seats away from the Northeast and Midwest.

Cultural effects

Migrants carry their language, religion, food, music, and building styles with them. This is relocation diffusion, which you will study fully in Unit 3. Its results show up in the cultural landscape: signs in two languages, mosques and temples alongside churches, and restaurants that become part of the local diet.

Common trap

"Migration affects the economy" earns nothing. An effect needs a direction, a place, and a group: "Emigration of nurses from the Philippines reduces the number of trained health workers available in rural Philippine provinces." Exam technique

If an FRQ asks for an effect of migration, check whether it specifies origin or destination, and political, economic, or cultural. Then answer exactly that box of the table above. A strong economic effect at the destination is worth nothing if the question asked for a cultural effect at the origin.

Check yourself · 2.124 items
Define remittances and explain one way they affect the migrant's country of origin.

Remittances are money that migrants send back to family or communities in their country of origin.

They raise household incomes, so families can pay for housing, food, school fees, and health care, or start small businesses. At the national scale they bring in foreign currency and can make up a large share of the economy — in some countries a quarter or more — which also makes the origin dependent on conditions in the destination's economy.

Explain how brain drain can affect a country that sends skilled migrants abroad.

When doctors, nurses, engineers, and other trained professionals emigrate for higher pay, the origin country loses the skills it paid to develop, often through public universities. Shortages follow — for example, fewer doctors and nurses in rural clinics — which can lower the quality of health care and slow development. Remittances and returning migrants can partly offset these losses.

Describe one cultural effect of migration on the destination's cultural landscape.

Immigrants change the visible landscape of the places where they settle: ethnic enclaves develop with shops, restaurants, places of worship, and signs in the migrants' language. Examples include Little Havana in Miami, with Cuban restaurants and Spanish signage, and the mosques and Arabic-language businesses of Dearborn, Michigan.

Explain how internal migration within the United States has had a political effect.

Seats in the U.S. House are reapportioned among states after each census according to population. Because Americans have migrated in large numbers to the South and West, states such as Texas and Florida have gained seats, while states in the Northeast and Midwest such as New York, Pennsylvania, and Ohio have lost them. After the 2020 census, Texas gained two seats. Since electoral votes equal House seats plus two, migration also shifts power in presidential elections.

Synthesis questions that cross topics

Stretch yourself · Unit 24 items
Explain how changes in women's roles (2.8) help move a country from Stage 2 to Stage 4 of the demographic transition model (2.5).

Stage 2 has a high birth rate and a falling death rate. Moving to Stage 3 and then Stage 4 requires the birth rate to fall. As girls stay in school longer, women marry later, enter paid work, and gain access to contraception, so they have fewer children. Lower infant mortality — itself helped by mothers' education — removes the need for extra births.

Together these changes bring the crude birth rate down toward the low death rate, closing the gap of natural increase. Women's changing roles are one of the main mechanisms that drive the DTM's Stage 3 decline in births.

Explain how immigration policy (2.7) can be used to respond to an aging population (2.9), and describe one effect on the destination's population pyramid (2.3).

An aging country has a high elderly dependency ratio and a shrinking workforce. By admitting more immigrants — through points systems, guest worker programs, or work visas — it adds young working-age adults immediately, which increases the number of workers and taxpayers supporting each retiree.

On the pyramid, immigration widens the bars for ages roughly 20–44, and if guest workers are mostly men, it widens the male side more than the female side. Over time, as immigrants have children, it can also widen the base.

Using carrying capacity (2.2) and Malthusian theory (2.6), explain why physiological density (2.1) is a better warning sign of population pressure than arithmetic density.

Malthus and the neo-Malthusians care about whether a population can be fed and supported — its relationship to carrying capacity. Arithmetic density divides people by all land, including deserts, mountains, and ice that grow no food, so it says little about that relationship.

Physiological density divides people by arable land only, so it measures how many people depend on each unit of food-producing land. A high physiological density signals a population close to the limits of its own farmland — one that must farm very intensively, import food, or risk the kind of shortfall Malthus warned about.

Explain how forced migration (2.11) can change the population composition (2.3) of both the origin and the destination.

Those who flee first and farthest are often young adults, especially young men, who are able to travel and may face conscription or violence. The origin is left with a population that is older and more female, with fewer working-age adults to rebuild. Its pyramid shows a notch at working ages.

Destinations near the conflict — often neighboring countries — receive large numbers of women and children as well, enlarging the youth cohorts and raising demand for schools and health care. Destinations farther away tend to receive more young men, widening the male side of the working-age bars.

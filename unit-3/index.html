Plate 3.1 Enduring understanding PSO-3

Introduction to Culture

PSO-3.ADefine the characteristics, attitudes, and traits that influence geographers when they study culture.

The big idea: culture is everything a group of people learns, shares, and passes on — how they eat, build, speak, worship, dress, and treat each other. None of it is inherited; all of it is taught. Geographers care because culture is never spread evenly: every trait has a where, and the where has an explanation.

Read to answer
  1. What exactly counts as culture, and what doesn't?
  2. What is a cultural trait, and what are the three kinds?
  3. How do single traits combine into whole cultural systems?
  4. What is the difference between judging a culture and understanding one?

What culture is

Culture is the shared practices, technologies, attitudes, and behaviors that a society transmits from one generation to the next. Three words in that definition carry the weight:

  • Shared. One person's habit is a quirk. A practice becomes culture when a group holds it in common — a language, a cuisine, a way of greeting strangers.
  • Learned. Nobody is born with a culture. A baby adopted across the world grows up fully fluent in the culture that raises it. That is why culture can change, mix, and spread — the subject of the rest of this unit.
  • Transmitted. Culture is passed down — through family, school, religion, law, and media. A fad that dies with its generation never finished becoming culture.

A cultural trait is a single distinguishable piece of a culture: one food, one tool, one rule, one belief. Geographers pay special attention to the traits that show up on the land — diet (what is grown, sold, and served), architecture (what buildings are made of and shaped like), and land use (what the space between buildings is for). Those three turn culture into something you can see from the street — which is Plate 3.2's whole subject.

Three kinds of trait: things, people, and ideas

Any trait fits one of three boxes, and the AP exam likes to make you sort them.

Key · The anatomy of culture3.1
Mentifact
A central, enduring element of a culture that lives in the mind: a belief, a value, a religion's core idea, a language's grammar. The hardest part of culture to change — and the slowest to diffuse.
Sociofact
The structures and organizations through which people relate to each other: families, governments, schools, religious institutions, sports leagues.
Artifact
The physical, visible objects a culture produces: tools, clothing, buildings, food, instruments. The easiest part of culture to trade, copy, and spread.
Norm
A spoken or unspoken rule about how to behave — how close to stand, when to arrive, what to wear to a funeral. Breaking a norm is awkward.
Taboo
A behavior so strongly forbidden by a culture that breaking it is unthinkable, not just rude — often involving food, death, or the body. Breaking a taboo is offensive or worse.

Traits rarely travel alone. A group of related traits — the beans, the pot, the morning routine, the café, the belief that no conversation starts without a cup — forms a culture complex around coffee. Complexes knit together into the whole fabric of a culture. This is why borrowing one trait from another culture often quietly imports several more that come attached to it.

Two attitudes toward difference

When geographers study a culture that is not their own, the CED names two opposite postures they can take.

Ethnocentrism is judging another culture by the standards of your own — and usually finding it inferior. Cultural relativism is evaluating a culture by its own standards: asking what a practice means to the people who practice it before judging it. Geographers work from cultural relativism, because ethnocentrism doesn't just offend — it produces wrong answers. If you decide a practice is irrational, you stop looking for its logic, and the logic is the explanation.

Common trap

Cultural relativism is a method, not a moral surrender. Students overcorrect and write that relativism means "no practice can ever be criticized." That is not what the exam wants. Relativism means you understand a practice in its own context first. On the exam, show the method: explain what the practice means inside the culture, using its own logic — don't praise or condemn it.

Connects to

Unit 1's sense of place is built out of cultural traits: the foods, sounds, buildings, and rituals that make a place feel distinct come straight from this plate. And the scale habit transfers whole: a "food culture" looks uniform at the national scale and splinters into regional cuisines the moment you zoom in.

Check yourself · 3.14 items
Define culture, and explain why the fact that culture is learned — not inherited — matters for the rest of this unit.

Culture is the shared practices, technologies, attitudes, and behaviors that a society transmits across generations.

Because culture is learned, it can be taught to outsiders, carried to new places, and changed. If culture were biological, it could not diffuse. Every process in Plates 3.4–3.8 — diffusion, acculturation, assimilation, syncretism — depends on people being able to learn traits they were not born into.

Classify each as an artifact, a sociofact, or a mentifact: a mosque's minaret; the belief that elders must be obeyed; a national soccer league.

The minaret is an artifact — a physical object the culture built. The belief about elders is a mentifact — it exists only as an idea, though you can see its effects. The soccer league is a sociofact — an institution that organizes how people relate to one another.

Watch for chains on the exam: a mentifact (the belief) usually explains a sociofact (the institution), which explains the artifact (the building). Being able to run that chain in either direction is the skill.

A traveler writes: "The food here is strange and the houses are built all wrong." Identify the attitude this shows, and rewrite the sentence as a cultural relativist would.

This is ethnocentrism: the traveler is measuring another culture's diet and architecture against their own culture's standards ("strange," "wrong" compared to what they know).

A relativist version asks what the traits accomplish in their own context: "The dishes here use fermentation to preserve food through the long winter, and the houses are raised on posts to stay dry in the flood season." Same observations — but now they produce explanations instead of verdicts.

Explain why geographers single out diet, architecture, and land use among all cultural traits.

Because those three traits are written onto the land itself, they give culture a visible spatial expression. A belief is invisible until it becomes a temple; a cuisine is invisible until it becomes rice paddies, market stalls, and restaurant signs.

Geography is the study of the where, so geographers lean on the traits that can be mapped, photographed, and compared from place to place. These visible traits are exactly what Plate 3.2 assembles into the cultural landscape.

Plate 3.2 Enduring understanding PSO-3

Cultural Landscapes

PSO-3.BDescribe the characteristics of cultural landscapes.
PSO-3.CExplain how landscape features and land and resource use reflect cultural beliefs and identities.

The big idea: land doesn't stay blank. Every group that lives in a place carves its culture into it — fields, temples, signs, street grids, monuments — until the place itself becomes a document. The cultural landscape is that document: the natural landscape as modified by human activity. Geographers learn to read it the way historians read a text, and the AP exam hands you photographs and expects you to do exactly that.

Read to answer
  1. What ingredients combine to make a cultural landscape?
  2. How can one place record several cultures, one on top of another?
  3. What do traditional and postmodern architecture each say about the culture that built them?
  4. How do attitudes about ethnicity and gender show up in the use of space?

Reading a landscape, ingredient by ingredient

The CED lists what a cultural landscape is made of. Treat this list as your checklist when a stimulus photo appears: run down it and name what you see.

Key · Landscape vocabulary3.2
Cultural landscape
The visible, human-modified surface of a place: the natural landscape plus everything culture has built, planted, carved, and named on it. Also called the built environment where the modification is construction.
Sequent occupancy
The idea that successive societies each leave their mark on the same place, so the landscape accumulates layers — a street grid from one era, place names from another, temples-turned-churches-turned-museums from a third.
Traditional architecture
Building styles that grow out of a place: local materials, forms fitted to climate and custom, designs repeated for generations (steep roofs where snow falls, thick earthen walls where it's hot and dry, stilts where it floods).
Postmodern architecture
Styles of roughly the 1960s onward that mix materials and borrow forms playfully from many eras and places — glass, steel, and references — expressing global wealth and identity more than local climate.
Ethnic neighborhood
An area where an ethnic group concentrates, marking the landscape with its own businesses, languages, houses of worship, and festivals.
Gendered space
Space whose use is shaped by ideas about gender — from separate prayer halls to the fact that more women working outside the home changes commuting patterns, childcare locations, and shop hours.
Common trap

"Natural" features belong in your answer too. Students skip the river, the hill, and the forest because they seem like the opposite of culture. But the CED's definition of a cultural landscape includes physical features — because culture decides what they mean. One culture's hill is grazing land; another's is a sacred summit no one may build on; a third's is a quarry. If the photo shows a physical feature, say what the culture is doing with it.

Layer on layer: sequent occupancy

Identity in the use of space

Learning objective PSO-3.C asks for something more specific than "culture shapes landscapes": it asks how attitudes toward ethnicity and gender show up in how space gets used.

  • Ethnic neighborhoods. Migrants cluster — by choice, for mutual support and familiar institutions, and sometimes by constraint, when discrimination limits where they can live. The result is a landscape-within-a-landscape: signs in another script, grocery stores stocking another cuisine, a house of worship anchoring the blocks around it.
  • Indigenous landscapes. Where Indigenous communities hold land, the landscape records a different relationship to it — communal management, sacred sites protected from development, place names that predate the colonial map. Where they lost land, the loss is legible too, in reservations' boundaries and renamed features.
  • Gender and space. A culture's attitudes about women's roles are visible on the land. As more women enter the paid workforce, landscapes respond: more childcare centers, different commuting flows, shops and services open at different hours. Where cultural or religious norms separate men's and women's spaces, buildings themselves are designed around that separation.
Exam technique

Landscape questions are two-step questions. Step one: name the visible feature ("the signs are in two languages"). Step two: connect it to the belief or identity it reflects ("— which shows a bilingual community large enough for businesses to court in both tongues"). The second step is where the point lives. A feature without an inference is a Describe answer to an Explain question.

Check yourself · 3.24 items
Define cultural landscape, and identify four distinct kinds of evidence the CED says it can contain.

A cultural landscape is the natural landscape as modified by human activity — the visible record of culture on the land.

Any four of: physical features given cultural meaning or use; agricultural and industrial practices; religious and linguistic characteristics (houses of worship, signage, toponyms); evidence of sequent occupancy; and architecture and land-use patterns, both traditional and postmodern.

Explain what sequent occupancy is, using a real or invented city as your example.

Sequent occupancy is the layering of successive societies' imprints on one place — each occupant modifies the landscape, and later occupants build over, around, and with those modifications rather than erasing them.

Mexico City works as the standard example: the Spanish built their colonial capital directly on Aztec Tenochtitlán — the cathedral sits beside the Templo Mayor's ruins — and the modern megacity grew over both, so one plaza exposes three layers at once. The key phrase to use: each layer still shapes the layers above it.

A photo shows houses with thick mud-brick walls, tiny windows, and flat roofs. Describe what the architecture suggests about the local environment, and explain why this counts as "traditional" architecture.

Thick earthen walls and small windows hold off intense heat by day and release it slowly at night, and a flat roof suggests little rain or snow to shed — so this is almost certainly a hot, dry climate.

It is traditional architecture because the form comes from the place itself: local materials (earth), a design fitted to the local climate, and a style repeated across generations rather than chosen from a global catalog. Contrast a postmodern glass tower, which could stand in any city on Earth and is built against the climate (it needs constant air conditioning) rather than with it.

Explain one way attitudes about gender can be read from a cultural landscape.

Strong answers tie a visible spatial pattern to the attitude behind it. Examples: a rising share of women in the paid workforce shows up as childcare centers near office districts and transit timed to two-earner commutes; norms of gender separation show up in buildings designed with separate entrances or prayer halls; a culture's attitude about who belongs in public space shows up in who the parks, sports fields, and coffeehouses are actually built for.

Formula: name the built feature, then name the attitude it encodes.

Plate 3.3 Enduring understanding PSO-3

Cultural Patterns

PSO-3.DExplain patterns and landscapes of language, religion, ethnicity, and gender.

The big idea: languages, religions, and ethnic groups are not scattered randomly — they cluster into regions. Those regions give people a shared sense of who and where they are. But the clusters almost never line up neatly with political borders, and in the gap between the cultural map and the political map live most of the world's unifying symbols and most of its conflicts.

Read to answer
  1. Why do cultural traits form regions at all?
  2. How do shared traits create a sense of place?
  3. When does a shared language or religion unite a society — and when does it divide one?
  4. Why is the mismatch between cultural regions and political borders so important?

Culture comes in regions

Because culture spreads from person to person and generation to generation, people near each other end up sharing traits. The result is the culture region — an area whose people share language, religion, ethnicity, or some bundle of all three. This is Unit 1's region concept doing Unit 3's work: a formal region drawn around a trait ("Spanish is spoken here"), or a vernacular one drawn around an identity people feel ("the South").

Traits trace back to a culture hearth — the place where a cultural practice or innovation began before spreading outward. Plates 3.4–3.7 are entirely about the spreading; for now, the key pattern is that the farther you travel from a hearth, the weaker and more mixed the trait tends to become.

Key · Pattern vocabulary3.3
Culture region
An area whose inhabitants share cultural traits such as language, religion, or ethnicity. Borders are fuzzy; regions overlap.
Culture hearth
The source area where a cultural trait or innovation originated and from which it diffused.
Ethnicity
Identity based on shared culture, ancestry, and history — language, religion, customs, and a story of common origin. Learned and chosen, unlike the physical traits race sorts by.
Ethnic enclave
A concentration of an ethnic group inside a larger settlement — usually an urban neighborhood built by migration.
Ethnic island
A rural concentration of an ethnic group that stays distinct over generations, often held together by land ownership and isolation.
Sense of place
The meaning and identity people attach to a location — built out of its cultural traits: sounds, foods, buildings, festivals, teams, accents.
Centripetal force
A trait or feeling that pulls a society together — a shared language, faith, or story that makes strangers feel like compatriots.
Centrifugal force
A trait or feeling that pushes a society apart — a cultural divide that makes fellow citizens feel like strangers.

The same trait can unite or divide

The CED's second point is the subtle one: language, religion, and ethnicity are not inherently unifying or divisive. The same trait does both, depending on who shares it.

TraitActs centripetally when…Acts centrifugally when…
Languagenearly everyone shares it: one tongue carries the schools, courts, media, and jokes of a whole society.a state contains two or more language regions: schooling, signage, and government in one language feel like domination to speakers of the other.
Religiona shared faith supplies common holidays, ethics, and gathering places — a calendar the whole society keeps together.a religious boundary runs through the state, or a state promotes one faith over others; religious difference stacked on top of other differences divides deepest.
Ethnicitythe state and the ethnic group roughly coincide, so national identity and ethnic identity reinforce each other.one ethnic group dominates a multiethnic state, or an ethnic homeland is split among several states.

Gender belongs in this plate too: cultures differ regionally in the roles, rights, and spaces they assign women and men, and those differences pattern the map — in workforce participation, in education, in who migrates (Unit 2's feminization of migration), and in the gendered landscapes of Plate 3.2. Exam questions on gender usually ask you to connect a regional pattern to its cultural cause rather than to recite statistics.

Common trap

Don't treat ethnicity, race, and nationality as synonyms. On this exam: ethnicity is learned, shared culture and ancestry; race is a social classification based on physical traits — a categorization imposed from outside rather than a culture carried from inside; nationality is legal belonging to a state. A person can hold one nationality, claim two ethnicities, and be racially classified three different ways in three different countries. Use the word the question uses.

Connects to

Unit 4 runs on this plate. Centripetal and centrifugal forces, nations versus states, ethnic separatism, and devolution all start from the mismatch in Figure 3.3a between cultural regions and political borders. Learn the map logic now and Unit 4 becomes review.

Check yourself · 3.34 items
Explain how shared cultural traits produce a sense of place, using a specific example.

A sense of place is built from the traits people experience together in a location: the language on the signs and in the streets, the smell of the regional cuisine, the architecture, the festivals and teams people rally around. Because the residents share these traits, the place feels distinct and theirs.

Any concrete example earns it: a city's historic quarter whose church bells, market stalls, and dialect make it unmistakable; a neighborhood whose storefront signs, groceries, and street festivals announce the community that built it.

Using Figure 3.3a, explain one way language could act as a centrifugal force in Keldora — and one policy that could make it more centripetal.

Centrifugal: if schools, courts, and broadcasting operate only in Language A, the B-speaking highland region experiences the state as belonging to someone else — fuel for resentment or separatism, sharpened because the same region also follows a different religion and has kin across the border.

More centripetal: official bilingualism — B-language schools, signage, and media in the highlands, B recognized in the constitution — lets B speakers see themselves inside the national identity instead of outside it. (Whether it fully works is a Unit 4 question; what matters here is the mechanism.)

Compare an ethnic enclave and an ethnic island.

Both are spatial concentrations of an ethnic group that keep its culture visible on the landscape. The difference is setting and scale: an enclave is urban — a neighborhood inside a city, usually created and refreshed by migration — whereas an ethnic island is rural, a farming community that stays distinct across generations, held together by land ownership and relative isolation.

One-sentence version for a Compare prompt: "Whereas an ethnic enclave is an urban neighborhood sustained by ongoing migration, an ethnic island is a rural community sustained by land and isolation."

Explain why cultural boundaries that fail to match political borders so often produce conflict.

A state's institutions — schools, laws, official language, holidays — have to be in some culture. When the political border contains more than one cultural region, the state's choices privilege one group's traits and slight another's, turning everyday institutions into centrifugal forces. When a cultural region is split between states, the group may identify with kin across the border more than with its own capital.

Either mismatch creates a loyalty that competes with the state — which is why the dashed line in Figure 3.3a predicts trouble better than the solid one.

Plate 3.4 Enduring understanding IMP-3

Types of Diffusion

IMP-3.ADefine the types of diffusion, including relocation and expansion — contagious, hierarchical, and stimulus.

The big idea: culture moves. Every trait on today's map got where it is by diffusion — spreading from its hearth to new people and places. There are exactly two ways a trait can travel: it can move with people who physically relocate, or it can spread between people who stay put. The second way comes in three flavors, and sorting scenarios into the right flavor is one of the most reliably tested skills in the course.

Read to answer
  1. What distinguishes relocation diffusion from every kind of expansion diffusion?
  2. How do contagious and hierarchical diffusion each pick their next adopters?
  3. What makes stimulus diffusion different from simply copying a trait?
  4. Given a scenario, how do you decide which type it is?

The family tree of diffusion

First split: does the trait travel because its carriers move? Relocation diffusion is the spread of a trait through the physical movement of the people who practice it — migrants carry their language, religion, and cuisine to the destination. The trait appears somewhere new, and (this is the tell) it may weaken or vanish at the origin if enough carriers leave.

Expansion diffusion is everything else: the trait spreads outward to new adopters while the people stay put — so the total number of people practicing it grows, and the hearth keeps it. Expansion comes in three types, distinguished by who adopts next.

Key · The types, with the test for each3.4
Relocation
The trait spreads because its practitioners physically move. Test: did people migrate? Example: a migrant community's language and cuisine appearing in a new country's cities.
Contagious
Expansion to nearly everyone nearby, person to person, like a cold. Test: does proximity decide who gets it next? Example: a new slang phrase sweeping through a school; an internet meme spreading to everyone online at once.
Hierarchical
Expansion that moves down a ladder of power, wealth, or city size — node to node — skipping what lies between. Test: does it hit big, connected places first? Example: high fashion debuting in a few global cities before reaching smaller ones; a music style jumping city to city before filling in.
Reverse hierarchical
The ladder climbed upward: a trait starts in small towns and spreads to bigger cities. Example: a retail chain perfecting its model in rural towns before entering metros; a country-music trend going national.
Stimulus
Expansion of the underlying idea, which gets adapted to local culture where the original form is rejected or impossible. Test: was the trait changed to fit? Example: a global burger chain selling chicken and veggie burgers in India, where most customers avoid beef — the fast-food idea diffused; the beef patty did not.
Common trap

Relocation vs. expansion is about the people, not the distance. Students write "it spread far away, so it's relocation." Wrong test. Ask instead: did the practitioners move (relocation), or did new people adopt the trait where they already live (expansion)? A video going viral across the whole planet is expansion — nobody moved. A religion carried across an ocean by a few hundred settlers is relocation — even though millions later adopt it where it lands.

Exam technique

Scenario questions give you a story and ask for the type. Run this order: 1. Did carriers migrate? → relocation. 2. Was the trait adapted or changed to fit the new culture? → stimulus. 3. Did it jump between big or connected places, skipping between? → hierarchical. 4. Did it wash over everyone nearby? → contagious. Check in that order, because stimulus scenarios usually also involve hierarchy or contagion, and the adaptation is what the question wants you to spot.

Check yourself · 3.45 items
Define relocation diffusion and expansion diffusion, and state the single question that separates them.

Relocation diffusion: a trait spreads through the physical movement of its practitioners from one place to another. Expansion diffusion: a trait spreads to new adopters while remaining strong at its hearth, so the total number of practitioners grows.

The separating question: did the people carrying the trait move? If yes, relocation; if the trait moved between people who stayed put, expansion.

A dance trend appears on a video app and within two weeks teenagers on every continent are doing it. Identify the type of diffusion and justify your answer.

Contagious diffusion (a form of expansion). No one migrated, and the trend did not move down a ladder of cities or elites — it spread person to person to essentially everyone exposed, at once, through direct contact on the platform.

Worth noting for harder questions: the internet lets contagious diffusion ignore physical distance — the "neighbors" are everyone in the network. If the question stressed that only celebrities or major influencers did it first and fans copied them, hierarchical would become defensible — justification is what earns the point.

Explain why a global coffee chain changing its menu to sell tea-based drinks in a tea-drinking country is stimulus diffusion rather than simple expansion.

In stimulus diffusion the underlying idea spreads while the specific form is rejected and adapted. Here the idea — the branded café as a "third place" to gather, order, and linger — diffused successfully. But the original core trait (espresso drinks) ran into a cultural barrier (an established tea culture), so the form was modified to local taste.

Simple expansion would mean the trait arriving unchanged. The adaptation under cultural resistance is the fingerprint of stimulus diffusion.

Describe how hierarchical diffusion can leave two nearby places with very different cultures, and name the Unit 1 concept this violates.

Hierarchical diffusion travels along connections between nodes — big cities, elites, networks — not across space evenly. A trend can reach a megacity and, decades later, still not reach a farming town fifty kilometers away, because the town sits below the ladder's lowest rung. Meanwhile the megacity shares the trend with other megacities on distant continents.

This cuts against Unit 1's distance decay (and Tobler's first law — near things being more related): for hierarchical diffusion, connectivity beats proximity. Contagious diffusion is the type that obeys distance decay.

A grocery chain builds its first stores in small rural towns, refines its model there, and only later opens in major cities. Identify the diffusion pattern.

Reverse hierarchical diffusion: the trait climbs the urban hierarchy from the bottom up — small towns first, then larger cities — the mirror image of ordinary hierarchical diffusion.

It still counts as expansion diffusion (adopters multiply; nobody migrates), and it is still hierarchical in logic — the ladder is just climbed in the other direction.

Plate 3.5 Enduring understanding SPS-3

Historical Causes of Diffusion

SPS-3.AExplain how historical processes impact current cultural patterns.

The big idea: today's cultural map was drawn largely by three historical engines — colonialism, imperialism, and trade. They explain the otherwise strange facts of the modern world: why Portuguese is spoken in Brazil but not in neighboring Peru, why Catholicism dominates the Philippines, why cricket fills stadiums in South Asia, and why whole new languages exist that were born on plantations and in port towns. To explain a current pattern, the exam usually wants a historical process.

Read to answer
  1. How do colonialism and imperialism differ, and how did each spread culture?
  2. What cultural traits did empires plant, and which types of diffusion (3.4) did they use?
  3. How does trade spread culture differently than conquest does?
  4. Where do lingua francas, pidgins, and creole languages come from?

Empire as a diffusion machine

Imperialism is a state's push to extend its power over other territories and peoples — by force, politics, or economics. Colonialism is the version where the power actually occupies and settles the territory, sending administrators and colonists and ruling it directly. Both moved culture on a scale nothing before them matched, and they used every diffusion type in Plate 3.4 at once:

  • Relocation: settlers, soldiers, missionaries, and administrators physically carried their language, religion, law, and architecture — and the Atlantic slave trade forcibly relocated millions of Africans, whose cultural traits (musical forms, foodways, religious practices) reshaped the Americas.
  • Hierarchical: the colonizer's culture was installed at the top — in capitals, courts, schools, and the local elite — and trickled down from there. That is why the colonial language often became the language of government and the upper class first, and of everyone else only later or never.
  • Stimulus: colonized peoples adapted imposed traits to local culture — blending imposed religions with older beliefs and local forms (the syncretism of Plate 3.8 is mostly colonial history's child).

The deepest current pattern this explains: the world map of official languages is a map of former empires. Spanish and Portuguese across Latin America; English in North America, South Asia, and much of Africa and the Pacific; French across West and Central Africa — each is the residue of colonial rule, kept after independence partly because a neutral outside language can sidestep rivalry among domestic language groups.

Trade: diffusion without conquest

Trade spreads culture more quietly. Merchants carry goods, and the goods carry traits: foods, technologies, art styles, words, and religions move along trade routes and mix in port cities and caravan towns. The Silk Roads moved silk, paper, and Buddhism across Asia; trans-Saharan caravans carried Islam (with salt and gold) into West Africa; ocean trade seeded port cities everywhere with mixed, outward-facing cultures. Where conquest imposes traits from above (hierarchical), trade lets them travel contact by contact (contagious and relocation) — and gives the receiving culture more choice about what to adopt.

When languages collide: lingua franca, pidgin, creole

Empire and trade kept throwing people together who shared no language. Three solutions emerged, and the exam tests the differences between them.

Key · Contact languages3.5
Lingua franca
An existing language adopted as a common tongue between groups with different native languages — chosen for business, trade, or administration. Swahili serves much of East Africa; English serves the world. A lingua franca is usually somebody's full native language already.
Pidgin
A simplified, improvised mix of languages invented for basic communication — small vocabulary, stripped-down grammar, and no native speakers. Born in ports, plantations, and markets where workers and traders had to make do.
Creole
What a pidgin becomes when children grow up speaking it as their first language: a complete natural language with full grammar and native speakers. Haitian Creole (French base with West African structures) is the classic case. The process is creolization — and it works for whole cultures, not just languages: blended cuisines, musics, and religions are cultural creoles.
Cultural imperialism
The imposition or dominance of one culture over others — historically by empires at gunpoint, today more often through media and markets (which is Plate 3.6's subject).
Common trap

"Lingua franca" does not mean "mixed language." A lingua franca is usually an ordinary, fully formed language (English, Swahili) that other groups adopt for communication. A pidgin is a new, simplified mixture with no native speakers; a creole is a mixture that gained native speakers. If the question says people use it between communities for trade, say lingua franca; if it says a new blended language emerged, say pidgin or creole — and let native speakers decide which.

Connects to

Unit 4 and Unit 7 both pick this up. Colonial borders drawn across cultural regions (Plate 3.3's mismatch problem) drive Unit 4's conflicts and devolution; colonial economies built to export raw materials shape Unit 7's patterns of development. When an FRQ asks "explain a historical cause," colonialism is the answer more often than any other single word — but it only scores when you name the mechanism.

Check yourself · 3.54 items
Explain how colonialism accounts for the current distribution of the Spanish language and the Catholic religion in Latin America. Name the diffusion types involved.

Spain colonized most of Central and South America in the 16th–19th centuries. Spanish settlers, officials, and missionaries physically carried their language and Catholicism across the Atlantic (relocation diffusion), installed them in colonial capitals, courts, schools, and missions, from which they spread down to the wider population (hierarchical diffusion), and over generations the traits became dominant and self-sustaining (expansion).

The pattern persists today because independence changed the rulers, not the installed language and religion — the institutions kept transmitting both.

Compare how trade and conquest spread culture.

Whereas conquest imposes a bundle of traits from above — the colonizer's language, religion, and law installed through government, schools, and force (hierarchical) — trade offers traits contact by contact along routes and in ports, letting receiving cultures choose, adapt, and blend what arrives (contagious and relocation, often ending in stimulus adaptations).

Result: conquest tends to produce sharp replacement and resentment; trade tends to produce gradual mixing — port cities with layered, cosmopolitan cultures, and religions like Islam in West Africa or Buddhism along the Silk Roads arriving with merchants before any army.

Define lingua franca, pidgin, and creole, and give the one-question test that separates a pidgin from a creole.

Lingua franca: an existing language adopted as a common tongue between groups with different native languages. Pidgin: a simplified blend of languages improvised for basic communication, with no native speakers. Creole: a pidgin that acquired native speakers and expanded into a complete language.

The test: does anyone grow up speaking it as a first language? No → pidgin. Yes → creole.

Many former colonies kept the colonizer's language as an official language after independence. Explain one practical reason why.

Most colonies contained many domestic language groups. Choosing one group's language as official would privilege that group and act as a centrifugal force (Plate 3.3). The colonial language, already installed in government, courts, and schools, was equally foreign to everyone — a neutral compromise that doubled as a window to international trade and diplomacy.

Nigeria (English) and Senegal (French) follow exactly this logic. Note the irony for your synthesis file: an instrument of cultural imperialism repurposed as a centripetal force.

Plate 3.6 Enduring understanding SPS-3

Contemporary Causes of Diffusion

SPS-3.AExplain how historical processes impact current cultural patterns — here, the contemporary processes: urbanization, globalization, and communication technology.

The big idea: the engines of diffusion changed. Empires and caravans moved culture over centuries; cities, markets, and screens move it in hours. Culture is socially constructed — people make and remake it constantly — and the contemporary forces of urbanization, globalization, and instant communication have cranked the remaking to a speed with no historical precedent. The results run in two directions at once: cultures grow more alike and more deliberately different.

Read to answer
  1. Why are cities such powerful cultural mixers and transmitters?
  2. Through what channels does globalization spread culture?
  3. What is time–space convergence, and what does it do to diffusion?
  4. When does global contact produce convergence, and when divergence?

Cities: where culture is made and launched

Urbanization — the growing share of people living in cities — concentrates strangers from many cultures in one place. That makes cities doubly important for this unit: they are mixing chambers, where contact between groups breeds new traits (new musics, cuisines, slangs, styles), and they are launch pads, sitting at the top of the urban hierarchy so that whatever catches on in them diffuses hierarchically to everywhere else. Most of what Plate 3.4 called hierarchical diffusion runs specifically through the network of big cities.

Globalization and the shrinking map

Globalization is the deepening interconnection of societies worldwide through trade, investment, migration, media, and technology. For culture, its key effect is captured by time–space convergence: the travel and communication time between places keeps falling, so places are effectively closer together than they used to be.

The channels the CED names are worth knowing as a list, because FRQs ask for them by name: media (films, television, streaming, and social platforms carry traits into homes worldwide), technological change (the internet and smartphones put a diffusion channel in every pocket), politics (states promote or censor cultural flows), economics (global brands and markets sell the same products, foods, and fashions everywhere), and social relationships (diaspora families, online communities, and travel keep distant people in daily contact).

Convergence — and its backlash, divergence

Point all those channels at the world for a few decades and cultures start to share traits: the same franchises, fashions, apps, music, and above all the same second language — English, the dominant global lingua franca of business, science, aviation, and the internet. Cultures becoming more alike through contact is cultural convergence.

But contact also produces the opposite. Cultural divergence is cultures becoming more distinct — sometimes because isolation keeps them apart, and sometimes as a deliberate response to globalization: communities restricting outside media, reviving traditional dress and festivals, or passing laws to protect a national language. Divergence is not a failure of globalization; it is one of globalization's effects.

What the new engines cost: vanishing languages

The CED singles out one consequence: as global languages spread through schooling, media, and economic opportunity, thousands of Indigenous and minority languages are falling silent — children raised in a dominant language never fully learn their grandparents' tongue, and when the last native speakers die the language dies. Linguists estimate that of the world's roughly 7,000 living languages, a large share — often put near half — are endangered. Each loss takes a mentifact system with it: oral histories, place knowledge, and ways of categorizing the world that existed nowhere else. Revival efforts — immersion schools, media in the language, official status — are divergence fighting for a language's life (Māori in New Zealand and Welsh in Wales are the standard success stories).

Common trap

Don't write that globalization simply "destroys local culture." It's a one-way claim about a two-way process, and it costs nuance points. Globalization spreads dominant traits and provokes divergence, and hands small cultures new tools — a diaspora can stream hometown radio, an endangered language can run an online immersion school, a local craft can sell worldwide. Strong answers show the push and the pushback.

Exam technique

When a question asks how contemporary processes spread culture, name the channel and the diffusion type together: "streaming media spreads traits by contagious diffusion, reaching all connected viewers at once" or "global brands spread hierarchically, opening in world cities first." The pairing shows you own both plates — 3.4's types and 3.6's engines.

Check yourself · 3.64 items
Define time–space convergence, and explain what it does to contagious diffusion.

Time–space convergence is the shrinking of effective distance between places as transportation and communication technologies cut the time needed to move people, goods, and information.

Contagious diffusion spreads through contact, and convergence redefines who is "in contact": online, everyone connected is effectively adjacent. So a trait that once rippled outward village by village can now wash over a global population in days — contagious diffusion at the planet's scale, with distance decay nearly flattened.

Explain two different ways urbanization drives cultural change.

Cities mix: urbanization concentrates people from many cultures in dense contact, and that contact generates new traits — blended cuisines, musical styles, slangs — making cities cultural hearths in their own right.

Cities transmit: sitting at the top of the urban hierarchy, cities launch whatever catches on in them down to smaller places through hierarchical diffusion. The two roles compound: cities invent more culture and spread it faster than anywhere else.

Give one example of cultural convergence and one of cultural divergence, and explain why both can be effects of globalization.

Convergence: English spreading as the global lingua franca — or the same streaming series, fashion brands, and apps appearing on every continent — as sustained contact makes cultures share more traits.

Divergence: a government legislating to protect its national language in broadcasting, or a religious community deliberately limiting outside media and technology.

Both trace to the same cause: globalization creates the contact. Contact spreads traits (convergence), and the pressure of those incoming traits is exactly what provokes communities to defend their distinctiveness (divergence). No contact, no pressure; no pressure, nothing to resist.

Explain how the spread of a global language can endanger Indigenous languages, and identify one strategy communities use against it.

Schooling, media, and jobs increasingly run in the dominant language, so parents and children face a practical incentive to use it — each generation uses the Indigenous language less, until children no longer learn it natively and transmission breaks. The language dies not by ban but by economics and media pulling every incentive one way.

Counter-strategies rebuild transmission and incentive: immersion schools that teach children in the language, media and apps in the language, and official status that gives it public value — the Māori and Welsh revivals being the standard examples.

Plate 3.7 Enduring understanding IMP-3

Diffusion of Religion and Language

IMP-3.BExplain what factors lead to the diffusion of universalizing and ethnic religions.

The big idea: the two heaviest pieces of any culture — its language and its religion — both spread outward from hearths, but not at the same speed or by the same rules. Languages branch like family trees as they travel. Religions split into two kinds with opposite travel habits: universalizing religions are built to spread, and ethnic religions are built to stay. Students rank this the hardest topic in the unit; the fix is to learn the logic of why each faith moved the way it did, not just the map.

Read to answer
  1. How are languages organized into families, branches, and dialects — and what does the tree have to do with diffusion?
  2. What can toponyms prove about a place's past?
  3. What separates a universalizing religion from an ethnic one?
  4. For each major religion: where is its hearth, and how did its beliefs shape its spread?

Languages travel like families

Languages are grouped by descent, exactly like a family tree. A language family is a set of languages sharing one distant ancestor; families split into branches, branches into individual languages, and languages into dialects — regional versions with their own vocabulary, accent, and grammar, still mutually understandable. The tree is a diffusion record: every split marks a moment when speakers moved apart (relocation diffusion) and their speech drifted until it divided.

Two names to know: Indo-European is the largest family — English, Spanish, Hindi, Russian, Persian, Bengali — with nearly half the world's speakers; Sino-Tibetan (Mandarin and its relatives) is second. And one skill: a toponym — a place name — is landscape evidence of language diffusion. A map of the United States says "New Orleans" (French), "Los Angeles" (Spanish), "Minneapolis" (Dakota + Greek): three colonial and Indigenous layers, all legible in the names alone. Sequent occupancy (3.2), written in letters.

Two kinds of religion, two travel habits

A universalizing religion holds that its message is for all humanity, actively seeks converts, and therefore spreads — by missionaries (expansion: contagious and hierarchical) and by migration (relocation). An ethnic religion is bound to one people and usually one place; it does not seek converts, so it stays concentrated near its hearth and moves only when its people move (relocation). The belief system itself is the diffusion engine — or the brake.

Key · Language & religion vocabulary3.7
Language family
Languages descended from a single common ancestor — Indo-European and Sino-Tibetan are the two giants.
Dialect
A regional variety of a language — distinct vocabulary, pronunciation, and grammar, but still mutually intelligible. An isogloss is a line on a map bounding where a word or usage is found.
Toponym
A place name — evidence of which cultures named, settled, or ruled a place, readable straight off the map.
Universalizing religion
Open to all humanity and actively seeking converts, so built for expansion diffusion: Christianity, Islam, Buddhism, Sikhism.
Ethnic religion
Bound to one people and usually one place; doesn't proselytize, so it stays near the hearth and travels only by relocation: Hinduism, Judaism.
Diaspora
A people — and their religion, language, and traits — dispersed from a homeland while keeping their identity, maintaining the culture far from its hearth.
Common trap

Universalizing does not mean big, and ethnic does not mean small. Hinduism (ethnic) has roughly twice the adherents of Buddhism and is vastly larger than Sikhism (both universalizing). The classification is about openness to converts and willingness to spread, not size. Second trap in the same topic: Buddhism's hearth. The exam loves that a universalizing religion can all but disappear from its birthplace while flourishing a continent away — if a map shows a religion thin at its origin and thick far away, think Buddhism.

Exam technique

Religion questions are really mechanism questions. Don't just say Islam "spread to Southeast Asia" — say who carried it and by which diffusion type: "Muslim merchants trading across the Indian Ocean brought Islam to Indonesian ports, where it spread by contagious diffusion — no conquest involved." Hearth + carrier + type is the full-credit formula.

Check yourself · 3.75 items
Explain why universalizing religions are distributed so much more widely than ethnic religions. Use one of each as evidence.

A universalizing religion believes its message applies to everyone, so it actively seeks converts — sending missionaries, welcoming outsiders — which powers expansion diffusion on top of relocation. An ethnic religion is tied to a particular people and place, doesn't proselytize, and often embeds its practice in a specific homeland, so it spreads only when its people migrate.

Evidence: Christianity (universalizing) spans every inhabited continent through missions and colonial expansion, while Judaism (ethnic), though ancient and globally scattered by diaspora, remains concentrated in Israel and the United States and seeks no converts. The belief system itself sets the travel rules.

Describe how Islam reached Indonesia, and explain why that example is worth memorizing.

Islam arrived in the Indonesian archipelago with Muslim merchants trading across the Indian Ocean, roughly from the 13th century on. It took root in port cities and spread inland by contact and conversion — relocation diffusion by traders, then contagious expansion, with local rulers' conversions adding a hierarchical push.

It's worth memorizing because it breaks the stereotype that Islam spread only by conquest — and because Indonesia is now the world's most populous Muslim-majority country, farther from Mecca than almost anywhere in the Muslim world. One example, three exam points: mechanism, counter-stereotype, current pattern.

Buddhism is universalizing, yet it is a minority religion in India, near its hearth. Explain how that happened and what it shows about diffusion.

Buddhism spread outward vigorously — rulers like Ashoka sponsored missionaries, and monks and merchants carried it along the Silk Roads into East and Southeast Asia (hierarchical and relocation diffusion). Meanwhile, at the hearth it was gradually reabsorbed and displaced over the centuries by Hinduism's revival and later by Islam's arrival.

What it shows: diffusion is not a tide that stays risen. A trait's presence at any place — including its own origin — depends on continuing transmission, and a religion can win the periphery while losing the core.

A region's map shows towns named by three different languages' naming patterns. Identify what geographers call these names, and explain what the pattern is evidence of.

These are toponyms — place names. Layered toponyms from different languages are evidence of sequent occupancy and past diffusion: each naming layer records a people who lived in or ruled the region long enough to put names on it — Indigenous names surviving under colonial ones, one empire's names over another's.

On an FRQ, treat toponyms like strata: name the languages, order the layers if you can, and state what each layer implies about who was there and when.

English and Hindi belong to the same language family. Explain what that claim means and how such a relationship is possible across so much distance.

It means both descend from a single ancestral language — Proto-Indo-European — so they sit on different branches (Germanic and Indo-Iranian) of one family tree, sharing deep structural and vocabulary roots.

The distance is the fossil of ancient relocation diffusion: speakers of the ancestor language migrated in different directions thousands of years ago, groups lost contact, and each group's speech drifted independently into new branches and languages. The family tree is a map of those long-finished migrations — which is why linguists can use language relationships to reconstruct prehistoric movement no document records.

Plate 3.8 Enduring understanding SPS-3

Effects of Diffusion

SPS-3.BExplain how the process of diffusion results in changes to the cultural landscape.

The big idea: diffusion ends in contact — and contact changes both cultures. The CED names four outcomes: acculturation, assimilation, syncretism, and multiculturalism. They are not four random vocabulary words; they are four points on one dial, set by a single question — how much of the original culture survives the meeting? Each outcome also rewrites the cultural landscape in its own recognizable way.

Read to answer
  1. What single question separates the four outcomes of cultural contact?
  2. How do acculturation and assimilation differ in degree — and in who changes?
  3. What makes syncretism a creation rather than a loss?
  4. What does each outcome look like on the landscape?

Four outcomes, one dial

Key · The four outcomes, with examples3.8
Acculturation
A group adopts some traits of another culture — usually the dominant one — while keeping its own identity. Immigrants who speak the new country's language at work and their own at home, keeping their cuisine, faith, and holidays, are acculturating.
Assimilation
A group gives up nearly all of its original traits and becomes culturally indistinguishable from the dominant society. It can happen gradually and voluntarily across generations — or be forced: government boarding schools in the U.S., Canada, and Australia once removed Indigenous children from their families expressly to strip their languages and cultures.
Syncretism
Two cultures' traits fuse into something new that belongs fully to neither parent. Mexico's Día de los Muertos blends Indigenous Mexican traditions with Catholic All Saints' and All Souls' Days; Haitian Vodou blends West African religions with Catholicism; a creole language (3.5) is syncretism you can hear.
Multiculturalism
Distinct cultures coexisting in one society — often backed by policy. Canada made multiculturalism official in 1971; Singapore runs four official languages. The landscape shows parallel institutions: multiple religious buildings, multilingual signage, many cuisines and festivals in one city.
Nativism
The backlash option: hostility toward immigrants and their cultures, demanding assimilation or exclusion. A reminder that outcomes of contact are contested, not automatic.

Reading the outcome off the landscape

Each outcome leaves its own signature on the cultural landscape — which is how a photo question can test this topic without using any of the four words:

  • Acculturation: an ethnic neighborhood whose shop signs are bilingual — the dominant language added, the original kept. Home and house of worship stay traditional; the storefront adapts.
  • Assimilation: the distinctive signs, buildings, and businesses fade over generations until the neighborhood looks like everywhere else; the old language survives only in a few toponyms.
  • Syncretism: buildings, festivals, and foods that visibly fuse two traditions — a church built with Indigenous iconography, a holiday blending two calendars, a cuisine naming two parents (Tex-Mex).
  • Multiculturalism: parallel landscapes in one city — a mosque, a church, and a temple within blocks; signage in several scripts; festival banners rotating through the year.
Common trap

Acculturation vs. assimilation is a question of degree — don't swap them. Acculturation = adopting some traits while keeping your own culture. Assimilation = losing the original culture into the dominant one. If the scenario says the group still keeps its language at home, its food, or its faith, it is acculturation, no matter how much else changed. And don't confuse syncretism with multiculturalism: syncretism fuses two cultures into one new trait; multiculturalism keeps them separate and intact side by side.

Connects to

This plate closes loops from the whole unit. The outcomes happen where Plate 3.4's diffusion delivers traits; colonial history (3.5) produced the world's great syncretisms — and its forced assimilations; migration (Unit 2) supplies the contact; and every outcome is legible in Plate 3.2's landscapes. A Unit 3 FRQ that starts in diffusion usually ends here.

Check yourself · 3.84 items
Compare acculturation and assimilation.

Whereas acculturation is adopting some of another culture's traits while retaining your own culture's core — identity, language at home, religion, cuisine — assimilation is the loss of the original culture as a group becomes absorbed into the dominant society.

Degree is the test: kept the core → acculturation; gave up the core → assimilation. Add, for an Explain prompt: acculturation is usually a two-way street in practice (the dominant culture picks up foods and words too), while assimilation is one-way — and has sometimes been forced by states.

Define syncretism, and use Día de los Muertos or another example to show why it counts as cultural creation.

Syncretism is the fusion of traits from two or more cultures into a new cultural trait that belongs fully to neither parent.

Día de los Muertos merges Indigenous Mexican traditions honoring the dead with the Catholic calendar's All Saints' and All Souls' Days: the result — ofrendas, marigolds, sugar skulls, family graveside celebration on November 1–2 — is not the Indigenous original and not the Spanish import. It is a third thing that exists only because the two met. That's why syncretism is counted as creation: diffusion didn't just move traits, it made one.

A city's historic immigrant neighborhood now shows only a few old street names and one annual festival from the founding group; everything else matches the wider city. Identify the outcome and the landscape evidence.

This is assimilation — nearly complete, a few generations on. The founding group's descendants have been absorbed into the dominant culture.

The evidence is what's missing from the landscape: no everyday signage in the old language, no distinctive businesses or institutions — only relict features, the toponyms (street names) and a commemorative festival. Relict traits like these are often the last visible trace a culture leaves on a landscape.

Explain how a government policy can push the outcome of cultural contact toward multiculturalism — or toward assimilation.

Toward multiculturalism: official recognition of multiple languages (schooling, signage, broadcasting), funding for minority cultural institutions and festivals, and legal protection of religious practice all let groups keep their cultures intact within one society — Canada's official multiculturalism policy is the standard example.

Toward assimilation: a single mandatory language of schooling, bans on minority-language instruction or dress, and — at the extreme — forced measures like the Indigenous boarding schools, which were explicitly designed to erase languages and cultures.

The point for the exam: the four outcomes are not natural fates; policy, power, and choice set the dial.

The distinctions the exam actually tests

Relocation vs. expansion

Did the carriers move?

People migrated → relocation. New adopters where they already live → expansion.

Contagious vs. hierarchical

Proximity or connection?

Ripples to every neighbor → contagious. Jumps big city to big city, skipping between → hierarchical.

Stimulus vs. the rest

Was the trait adapted?

The idea arrives but the form changes to fit local culture → stimulus.

Universalizing vs. ethnic

Does it seek converts?

Open to all and missionary-minded → universalizing. Tied to one people and place → ethnic. Size proves nothing: Hinduism is ethnic and enormous.

Acculturation vs. assimilation

Is the core kept?

Adopts some traits, keeps identity → acculturation. Original culture lost → assimilation.

Syncretism vs. multiculturalism

Fused or side by side?

Two cultures make a new third thing → syncretism. Both persist intact in one society → multiculturalism.

Pidgin vs. creole

Native speakers?

Improvised mix nobody grows up speaking → pidgin. Children learn it first → creole.

Ethnocentrism vs. relativism

Whose standards?

Judging by your own culture's rules → ethnocentrism. By the culture's own logic → relativism.

Synthesis questions that cross topics

Stretch yourself · Unit 34 items
Explain how colonialism (3.5) accounts for both the distribution of a universalizing religion (3.7) and a case of syncretism (3.8) in the same world region.

Take Latin America. Spanish and Portuguese colonization carried Catholicism — a universalizing religion — across the Atlantic by relocation diffusion, then spread it through missions, colonial institutions, and conversion (hierarchical and contagious expansion). That is why Latin America is overwhelmingly Christian today.

The same contact produced syncretism: imposed Catholicism fused with Indigenous traditions, producing blends like Día de los Muertos — the Catholic calendar carrying Indigenous practices of honoring the dead. One historical process, two exam answers: the distribution and the fusion.

Explain how time–space convergence (3.6) changes the balance between contagious and hierarchical diffusion (3.4).

Historically, hierarchical diffusion outran contagious diffusion because connection beat proximity: traits moved between linked cities by ship, rail, and broadcast while contagious spread crawled outward at the speed of personal contact.

Time–space convergence — especially the internet — puts nearly everyone in direct contact, so contagious diffusion now operates at global scale instantly: a meme reaches a village and a megacity in the same hour, no urban ladder required. Hierarchies still matter (influencers, platforms, world cities launch trends), but the rural lag that defined hierarchical diffusion keeps shrinking wherever connectivity reaches.

Using the mismatch between cultural regions and political borders (3.3), explain how a state's language policy could push a minority toward assimilation or toward multiculturalism (3.8) — and what each choice does to centripetal and centrifugal forces.

A one-language policy — schooling, courts, and media only in the majority tongue — pressures the minority toward assimilation: each generation uses the home language less until it fades. The state intends a centripetal result (one shared language), but imposed assimilation often works centrifugally instead, convincing the minority the state belongs to someone else.

Official bilingualism and funded minority institutions push toward multiculturalism: the culture persists intact inside the state. That concedes permanent difference but can act centripetally — the minority sees itself inside the national identity. The exam point: policy sets the dial of Plate 3.8, and the dial setting feeds back into Plate 3.3's forces.

A religion is thinly represented at its hearth but dominant far away; a language is spoken by a billion people but almost nowhere outside its hearth region. Explain how each pattern arose, naming the diffusion processes involved.

The religion is Buddhism's pattern. A universalizing religion expanded outward — royal sponsorship (hierarchical), monks and merchants along trade routes (relocation and contagious) — while at the hearth it was gradually displaced by resurgent and arriving faiths. Expansion abroad plus erosion at home = dominant periphery, thin core.

The language fits Mandarin's pattern (or Hindi's): an enormous population grew within the hearth region by natural increase, while limited mass emigration and no colonial empire carrying it abroad meant little relocation diffusion. Huge where it started, scarce elsewhere. Same map-reading lesson both ways: current distribution = hearth + diffusion history, never size alone.
